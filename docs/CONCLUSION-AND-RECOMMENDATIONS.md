# Conclusion

The study achieved its primary goal of developing and evaluating TulongGuro, a VLM-integrated LMS gradebook that automates qualitative, rubric-aligned feedback for Grade 6 English formative assessments while reducing the grading workload carried by DepEd teachers. The first three objectives were realized as working components of a deployed artifact: a server-side image-optimization module performing orientation correction, resolution capping, and JPEG compression before inference; a cloud-based Vision-Language Model integration that reads a photographed handwritten output and evaluates it against the rubric in a single inference step; and a multi-role LMS gradebook comprising a Teacher Dashboard built around Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) review, a Student Dashboard for the delivery of validated grades, feedback, and reciprocal literacy interventions, and an Admin Dashboard for school, account, curriculum, and rubric-template management.

The technical and usability evidence supports the successful implementation of these components within the evaluated setting. All 32 unit and component test cases and all five VLM functional scenarios returned a Pass verdict, and functional and regression testing closed at a 100% pass rate after the 13 problems identified among 170 test cases were resolved and re-verified. The system obtained an overall weighted score of 4.35 out of 5.00 on the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 evaluation, interpreted as Very Good, and the Data Privacy Implementation Assessment returned an overall attestation of "Compliant with Recommendations," with 21 of 27 assessed controls addressed and six identified as future enhancements tied to institutional deployment rather than to defects in the build. The System Usability Scale yielded scores of 84 for the Admin interface (Excellent), 80 for the Teacher interface, and 75 for the Student interface (both Good), indicating that each role-specific interface was perceived as usable by the user group it was designed for. The three curriculum-aligned rubrics used throughout the AI-assisted evaluation were themselves validated at a grand mean of 4.84, interpreted as Strongly Agree, establishing that the grading criteria applied by the system were judged relevant, clear, appropriate, and suitable by the domain experts.

The fourth objective produced the study's most consequential finding. When the raw, unreviewed AI-generated grade for each of the 35 assessment outputs was classified into the DepEd Order No. 8, s. 2015 descriptor bands and compared against the teacher-established reference grade, exact band agreement reached only 25.71% (9 of 35), with a macro-averaged precision of 12.27%, recall of 11.96%, and F1-score of 11.64%. The two assessment activities deviated in opposite directions: the Story Elements and Essay outputs were scored above the teacher reference (MD = +2.09; normalized MAE = 20.91%), while the Formal Persuasive Essay outputs were scored below it (MD = −5.46; normalized MAE = 10.92%). The absence of a single consistent bias indicates that the model's scoring behavior is conditioned by the assessment task, rubric structure, and response characteristics rather than by a uniform leniency or severity. The study therefore concludes that the VLM component, although functionally correct, does not independently reproduce professional grading judgment, and that TulongGuro is correctly positioned as an AI-assisted decision-support and feedback system rather than as an autonomous grader. This conclusion validates rather than undermines the HITL architecture: the design constraint that no AI-drafted score reaches a learner without teacher validation is not a precaution added to the system but the condition under which the system's outputs are defensible.

The value TulongGuro demonstrably delivers therefore lies in throughput and feedback quality rather than in autonomous accuracy. Mean grading time fell from 5.05 to 2.22 minutes per paper (55.34% reduction), a complete class set from 143.33 to 75.56 minutes (47.99%), and turnaround time from submission collection to the return of validated feedback from 2.55 to 1.50 days (41.16%), with the AI itself requiring a mean of 24.83 seconds per paper. Critically, the shorter turnaround was not achieved by returning less: the manual condition typically produced a numerical mark, whereas the TulongGuro-assisted condition returned a validated grade together with criterion-level qualitative feedback and a personalized reading-comprehension strategy. The participating teachers rated the AI-assisted grading and feedback functions at an overall mean of 4.48 (Strongly Agree), with Pedagogical Relevance rated highest at 4.60 — an evaluation rendered with full knowledge that the drafts required correction. Taken together, these results indicate that a model that cannot be trusted to assign the final mark can nonetheless be trusted to prepare the material from which a teacher assigns it, and that this division of labor is where the measured benefit originates.

These conclusions hold within clearly bounded conditions. The grading comparison drew on 35 outputs from two activities, the workload comparison on three teachers grading the same outputs sequentially, and the usability results on three teachers, three administrators, and thirty students across the three participating schools; the findings are consequently descriptive and are not generalizable to all Grade 6 English assessments, teachers, or schools. Processing time and grading behavior likewise remain contingent on handwriting legibility, response length, rubric complexity, batch size, network conditions, and the policies, pricing, and uptime of an external VLM provider that lies outside the control of the researchers or the participating schools.

Within those bounds, the study contributes a working demonstration that AI-assisted formative assessment is feasible under conditions that most existing Learning Management Systems do not accommodate: large class sizes, paper-based outputs, intermittent connectivity, and a mixed-device rather than one-to-one classroom. Its wider contribution is architectural as much as technical — it shows that the practical question in educational AI is not whether a model can grade as well as a teacher, which on this evidence it cannot, but how a system can be built so that an imperfect model still returns hours to the teacher and better feedback to the learner while leaving final academic authority exactly where professional and ethical practice requires it to remain.

# Recommendations

On the basis of the foregoing findings, the following recommendations are offered for the further development of the system, for future research, and for the schools and agencies that may adopt it.

## For System Development

**1. Implement a transactional email service for account provisioning and credential management.** The present implementation provisions teacher and student accounts entirely through the Admin Dashboard, with credentials communicated to users outside the system and password resets performed manually by a School Administrator or platform operator. This places an avoidable administrative burden on the Admin role and leaves the recovery path for a forgotten password dependent on a person rather than on a process. It is recommended that a verified transactional email channel be integrated so that (a) newly created teacher and student accounts receive their access credentials and an activation link directly, (b) users can initiate a self-service password reset through a time-limited, single-use token, and (c) school-registration approvals and rejections issued from the platform console are communicated automatically to the applying institution. Attaching a verified email address to each teacher account also supplies the identity anchor required for the multi-factor authentication recommended in item 5.

**2. Extend the email channel to assessment notifications for teachers.** Beyond account management, a working email channel should be used to notify teachers when a batch of submissions has finished AI processing and is ready for HITL review, when a submission fails AI processing and requires manual grading, and when submissions arrive within or after the configured late window. Because teachers in the evaluated setting do not remain logged into the platform between grading sessions, these events are presently discovered only on the next visit to the dashboard; delivering them by email would allow the review step to be scheduled around the teacher's day rather than the teacher's day around the dashboard, and would further compress the turnaround time already reduced by 41.16%.

**3. Develop a domain-adapted grading model trained on the system's own corpus of teacher-validated assessments.** The 25.71% band agreement was obtained from a general-purpose, third-party cloud VLM prompted with the rubric, and the study's delimitations acknowledge that the system's performance, operating cost, and data handling are partly contingent on an external provider. It is therefore recommended that the accumulated record of teacher-validated gradings — the paired AI draft and final teacher score already captured by the HITL workflow, together with the correction examples fed back into prompt calibration — be developed into a curated training corpus, and that this corpus be used to fine-tune or train a domain-adapted model for Grade 6 English rubric scoring. Such a model would be expected to reduce the task-dependent scoring deviation observed across the two assessment types, lower the per-paper inference cost that constrains deployment at DepEd scale, and reduce exposure to a third party's pricing, uptime, and policy changes. This recommendation carries explicit prerequisites: a corpus of sufficient size and diversity, which the present study's 35 outputs do not approach; documented consent and a Privacy Impact Assessment covering the secondary use of learner work for model training; de-identification of all training material; and access to training and hosting infrastructure. A staged path is advised, in which retrieval-based calibration on validated examples is exhausted first, parameter-efficient fine-tuning of an open-weight vision-language model is attempted next, and full in-house training is considered only once the corpus and the institutional mandate justify it. Locally hosted inference would additionally allow learner data to remain within national jurisdiction, an increasingly material consideration for a public-sector deployment.

**4. Improve grading calibration on a per-assessment basis.** The opposing deviation directions observed across the two activities indicate that a single global prompt configuration does not serve all assessment types equally. Calibration should be maintained per activity type and per rubric — anchored on teacher-validated exemplars at each descriptor band for that specific rubric — rather than applied uniformly, and the effect of each calibration change should be measured against a held-out set of teacher-graded papers before it is released to production.

**5. Strengthen the security and safety characteristics identified as lowest in the technical evaluation.** Security (3.75) and Safety (3.80) received the lowest ISO/IEC 25010:2023 sub-scores, and six of the 27 privacy controls were deferred as future enhancements. Prior to any deployment beyond a pilot, it is recommended that multi-factor authentication be added for Admin and platform-operator accounts, that authentication attempts be rate-limited, that backup and recovery procedures be formalized and tested, that data retention and disposal schedules be documented as institutional operating procedure rather than implemented only in code, and that a full Privacy Impact Assessment and third-party integration review be completed for the external VLM provider.

**6. Quantify the image-optimization module's benefit directly.** The first objective was established at the implementation level, but the present evaluation did not independently measure the bandwidth and API-token reduction produced by orientation correction, resolution capping, and compression. A controlled benchmark comparing payload size, transfer time, token consumption, and resulting grade stability with and without optimization would convert an implemented feature into a measured one, and would supply the cost-per-paper figure that any school-level procurement decision requires.

**7. Address the onboarding variability observed in the usability results.** Item Q10 of the SUS, concerning the amount of learning required before use, produced the widest dispersion in all three respondent groups (Teacher M = 3.33, SD = 2.08; Admin M = 2.67, SD = 2.08; Student M = 2.67, SD = 1.12), as did Q4 on the need for technical assistance. An in-application guided walkthrough for first-time users of each role, contextual help within the HITL review screen, and a printable one-page quick-start guide for teachers and administrators are recommended to narrow this dispersion before wider rollout.

## For Future Research

**8. Replicate the AI reliability evaluation under matched rating conditions.** The comparison reported here placed a strictly rubric-constrained rater (the AI) against teacher-established reference grades, and future work should ensure strict instrument equivalence by requiring that the teacher grade the same papers using the identical rubric and enter those marks through the HITL review screen. Doing so removes rater-instrument mismatch as a competing explanation for the observed disagreement, and carries the additional benefit of feeding the resulting correction pairs into the system's calibration loop, which records nothing when grading is performed on paper. Subsequent studies should also report inter-rater reliability measures appropriate to ordinal descriptor bands, such as quadratic-weighted Cohen's kappa or the intraclass correlation coefficient, alongside raw band agreement, since exact-match accuracy penalizes an adjacent-band deviation as heavily as a distant one.

**9. Scale the evaluation dataset and the teacher sample.** A dataset substantially larger than 35 outputs, drawn from more than two activities and covering the full range of supported assessment formats, is required before any claim about grading reliability can be generalized. Similarly, a teacher sample larger than three, drawn from multiple schools and divisions, is required before the observed workload reduction can be treated as anything other than descriptive.

**10. Adopt a counterbalanced workload design.** The manual and TulongGuro-assisted conditions in this study used the same outputs in fixed sequence, admitting a familiarity and order effect. Future work should randomize or counterbalance the order of conditions, use non-overlapping paper sets, and add a blind expert rating of feedback quality in both conditions, so that time saved and feedback quality are measured against each other rather than reported separately.

**11. Compare performance across supported input types and network conditions.** The processing-time results principally represent photographed handwritten outputs. A direct comparison of handwritten, typewritten PDF, and .docx submissions — and of performance under varying connection quality, including recovery through the offline upload queue — would establish whether the reported figures hold across the full submission surface the system claims to support.

**12. Study the calibration loop longitudinally.** Because teacher corrections are captured as calibration examples, agreement between AI drafts and teacher judgments is expected to improve as the corpus of validated gradings grows. A longitudinal study measuring band agreement across successive grading cycles within the same class would establish whether this improvement occurs, at what rate, and whether it transfers across teachers, rubrics, and schools.

**13. Investigate learning outcomes rather than workflow outcomes alone.** The present study measured the production of feedback, not its effect. Research examining whether the compressed turnaround and the criterion-level qualitative feedback measurably improve Grade 6 learners' writing and reading comprehension over a grading period would test the pedagogical premise on which the system rests.

## For Schools, Administrators, and the Department of Education

**14. Deploy initially as a supervised pilot governed by an explicit HITL policy.** Adopting schools should institute a written policy stating that no AI-generated score is released to a learner or recorded in official grades without teacher validation, mirroring the constraint the system enforces technically. Given the measured band agreement, treating an AI draft as a final mark would be indefensible, and school policy should state this explicitly rather than rely on the interface alone.

**15. Locate the platform-operator role within DepEd.** Approving school registrations and overseeing administrator accounts across institutions is a governance function rather than a technical one. In this study the researchers operated that console as a stand-in for an eventual DepEd-held role; in an operational deployment it should be held by the appropriate DepEd division or regional office, which would also carry the accompanying data-controller responsibilities.

**16. Provide teacher orientation and a learner- and guardian-facing AI-use notice.** Adopting schools should conduct a short orientation covering the HITL review workflow, the correct interpretation of an AI draft, and the circumstances under which the AI should be overridden, and should issue a plain-language notice to guardians describing what learner work is processed, by which external service, and for how long it is retained.

**17. Budget for per-paper inference cost and preserve the separation of AI budgets.** Operating cost scales with the number of papers processed rather than with the number of user licenses, and adopting institutions should plan procurement accordingly using the cost-per-paper figure recommended in item 6. The existing separation between the grading pool and the teacher-assistant pool should be preserved in any scaled configuration, so that assistant usage cannot exhaust the budget on which the checking queue depends.

**18. Extend to other grade levels and subjects only after per-domain rubric validation.** The system is delimited to Grade 6 English rubric-scored outputs, and the rubric validation that grounds its grading criteria was conducted for that domain. Any extension to another grade level or subject should repeat both the rubric validation and the AI reliability evaluation for that domain before operational use, rather than assuming transfer from the results reported here.
